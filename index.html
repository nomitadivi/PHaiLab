Public Information & Awareness

New World
Screwworm
Know It. Prevent It. Report It.

New World Screwworm (NWS) is a parasitic fly that primarily affects livestock and animals. It has moved northward through Central America and Mexico since 2023 and recently been detected in South Texas. Early detection and reporting are our most powerful tools.

📖 Learn the Basics
The Basics

What Is New World Screwworm?

Understanding what it is, how it works, and why early action matters.

🪰

It's a Fly — Not a Worm

Despite the name, New World Screwworm (Cochliomyia hominivorax) is actually the larva of a fly — about the size of a housefly with orange eyes and a shiny blue-green body with three dark stripes on its back. Most people can't tell it apart from other flies.

🥚

How It Starts

Female flies lay 200–300 eggs near open wounds or body openings (nose, ears, eyes, mouth, genitals) on living, warm-blooded animals. Even a tiny scratch or tick bite can attract a fly. One female can lay up to 3,000 eggs in her lifetime.

🐛

The Larvae Burrow In

Eggs hatch into cream-colored, screw-shaped larvae (maggots) that burrow into living tissue — not dead material. This is what makes NWS so different from ordinary fly larvae. They feed for about 7 days, deepening the wound.

🌎

Where It's Found

NWS is native to warmer parts of the Western Hemisphere — Cuba, Haiti, Dominican Republic, and South America. After being eradicated from the U.S. in 1966, it's been moving northward again since 2023. A small number of cases have recently been confirmed in South Texas.

🏆

We've Beat It Before

The U.S. successfully eradicated NWS in 1966 using the Sterile Insect Technique — releasing sterile flies to prevent reproduction. A 2016–2017 outbreak in the Florida Keys was also handled successfully. Experts have proven tools and knowledge to combat this.

🐄

Mostly Animals — Rarely People

NWS mainly affects livestock and wildlife. Human cases are uncommon and have not been locally acquired in the U.S. When found in people, it's very treatable — especially when caught early. The food supply (meat, milk, eggs) is not at risk.

The Life Cycle of a Screwworm Fly

1

Eggs Laid

Female lays 200–300 eggs on a wound or body opening

2

Eggs Hatch

Larvae emerge within 12–24 hours and immediately begin burrowing

3

Larvae Feed

Burrow into living tissue for ~7 days, enlarging the wound

4

Drop to Soil

Mature larvae drop to the ground and burrow into the soil to pupate

5

Adult Fly Emerges

Adult flies emerge in 7–54 days, ready to mate and restart the cycle

Signs & Symptoms

What to Watch For

Knowing the signs — in people or animals — helps you act fast. Early treatment makes a big difference.

🐄 In Animals

  • A wound that isn't healing or is getting bigger
  • Unusual drainage, discharge, or foul odor from a wound
  • Visible maggots (larvae) in or around a wound
  • Animal seems uncomfortable, won't eat, or keeps to itself
  • Swelling, redness, or inflammation around a wound
  • Newborns: check the navel carefully in first days of life
  • New mothers: check the birth area; males check the sheath

👤 In People

  • A wound or sore that isn't healing and worsens over a few days
  • Feeling of movement within a wound or sore
  • Visible larvae (maggots) in a wound, ear, nose, or mouth
  • A foul-smelling odor from the site of infestation
  • Intense or increasing pain at the wound site
  • Bleeding from open sores
  • Fever or chills (may indicate a secondary infection)
🚨 If you suspect NWS in a person: Contact a doctor or urgent care immediately. Do not try to remove larvae yourself — let a healthcare provider do this safely. If it feels like an emergency, call 911. For a health center near you, call the NCFH Call for Health Helpline: 1 (800) 377-9968 (also available via WhatsApp at 1-737-999-4162 — English & Spanish).
🐄 If you suspect NWS in an animal: Keep the animal in place. Do not discard any larvae. Call your veterinarian AND your state animal-health official right away. They'll guide next steps and collect a sample for lab confirmation.
Prevention

Simple Steps That Make a Difference

Prevention is mostly common sense. Fewer wounds + quick wound care + awareness = powerful protection.

🩹

Cover All Wounds

Keep any cut, scrape, or surgical site clean and covered, no matter how small. Even a tick bite can attract a female fly to lay eggs. This is the single most effective personal precaution.

🛏️

Sleep Protected

Sleep indoors with closed or screened windows. If outside, use a bed net or screened tent — especially in areas with active cases or warm seasons.

👕

Cover Up Outdoors

Wear loose-fitting long-sleeved shirts and pants, hats, and socks to minimize skin exposure when spending time outside in risk areas.

🧴

Use Insect Repellent

Apply any EPA-registered insect repellent as directed. Consider treating clothing and gear with 0.5% permethrin for an additional layer of protection against biting insects.

🚫

Dispose of Larvae Safely

Never throw live maggots in the trash or outside — this could allow the fly to complete its lifecycle. Place them in a sealed container with rubbing alcohol and bring to your healthcare provider.

✈️

Returning Travelers

If you've recently traveled to Mexico, Central America, or the Caribbean, monitor any wounds for 2 weeks. If a wound starts worsening instead of healing, see a doctor and mention your travel history.

For Livestock Producers & Ranchers

Protecting Your Animals and Your Operation

If you raise cattle, sheep, goats, horses, or other livestock, these steps can help protect your herd and your livelihood. Early detection and reporting are your best defenses.

🔍

Inspect Animals Regularly

Check all wounds regularly, plus natural body openings: nose, ears, eyes, and genitals. For newborns, inspect the navel closely in the first days of life. For new mothers, check the birth area. For males, check the sheath.

🩺

Treat Wounds Immediately

Treat every wound — no matter how small — as soon as you notice it. Treat newborn navels right after birth. Talk to your vet about approved wound treatment products for your species.

📅

Time Procedures Carefully

During higher-risk periods or near active cases, postpone optional procedures like dehorning, castration, or branding when possible. Work with your vet to schedule these in cooler, lower-risk seasons.

🦟

Control Biting Insects

Manage ticks and biting insects on your animals — these create the wounds that attract NWS flies. Eliminate sharp edges in pens and paddocks that could cause injury. Keep facilities clean.

💊

Know Your Treatment Options

Several products are authorized for prevention or treatment in cattle, dogs, and cats. Consult your veterinarian for current approved options and the right choice for each animal species. Do not use products without vet guidance.

📞

Report Immediately

If you see a wound with maggots, don't wait. Keep the animal in place, don't discard larvae, and call your vet and state animal-health official right away. Reporting protects your neighbors too.

⭐ Kids Corner ⭐

Learn About the Screwworm Fly!

A fun, friendly guide for young learners ages 6–12 — find out what this tiny bug is, why it matters, and how YOU can help!

🪰

Wait — It's a FLY?

Yep! Even though it's called a "screwworm," it's actually a tiny fly! The fly lays eggs in small cuts on animals, and the baby flies (called larvae) look a little bit like tiny screws — that's how they got the name. Kind of like how a caterpillar is the baby version of a butterfly!

🐄

Who Does It Affect?

Mostly farm animals like cows, sheep, goats, and horses. Sometimes deer and other wild animals too. People can very rarely get it, but it's not common at all. You definitely cannot catch it from another person, like you can a cold!

🦸

Scientists Are the Heroes!

Way back in the 1960s, scientists figured out a really clever trick: they released lots of special flies that couldn't have babies. Those flies tricked the other flies into not having babies either — and it WORKED! The U.S. got rid of the screwworm completely! Scientists are working hard to do that again.

🩹

How Can YOU Help?

It's easy! Keep your scrapes and cuts clean and covered with a bandage. If you live on a farm or ranch, help grown-ups check animals' wounds. If you see any animal with a weird-looking cut, tell an adult right away. You can be an animal protector!

⭐ Super Cool Science Fact! ⭐

Scientists got rid of the screwworm fly without using harmful chemicals! They used something called the Sterile Insect Technique — they made male flies unable to have babies, then released millions of them. The female flies mated with these special males, and no eggs could hatch. It's like a scientific superpower — and it worked for over 50 years!

🌟 Kid's Safety Tips — Easy to Remember!

  • 🩹Always put a bandage on cuts and scrapes — even small ones. Keep them clean!
  • 🧼Wash your hands after playing outside, especially on farms or near animals.
  • 👀If you see an animal with a weird smelly wound or acting strange, tell a grown-up right away.
  • 🐛If you ever see wiggly white maggots in a wound — don't touch them! Get a grown-up immediately.
  • 🌙Sleep indoors with windows closed or screened — it helps keep flies outside where they belong.
  • 📞If someone is sick from a bug wound, call a doctor. It's always better to be safe and get help!
Common Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers to the questions we hear most often.

Is screwworm dangerous to my family? +
Human cases are uncommon, and there have been no locally acquired human cases in the U.S. It mostly affects animals. When it does occur in a person, it is very treatable — the key is seeing a doctor promptly if you notice a wound worsening unexpectedly. Simple wound care (keep cuts clean and covered) keeps your personal risk very low.
Can screwworm spread from animal to animal — or person to person? +
No. Screwworm does not spread directly from animal to animal or person to person like a contagious disease. Each infestation starts fresh when a fly lays eggs on a wound. You can't "catch" it from being near an infested animal or person.
Is the food supply safe? Can I get it from eating meat? +
Yes, the food supply is safe. Screwworm does not infest meat, milk, eggs, fruits, or vegetables. The larvae only affect living tissue. Inspected food products remain safe.
What should I do if I find maggots on my animal? +
Stay calm. Keep the animal in place — do not move it out of the area. Do not discard any larvae or maggots. Call your veterinarian and your state animal-health official right away. They will guide you through next steps, including how to collect a sample for laboratory identification.
Should I try to remove maggots from a wound myself? +
For animals: do not attempt to treat the wound yourself. Contact your vet immediately — they'll use approved products and techniques. For people: do not attempt self-removal. Visit a healthcare provider so this can be done completely and safely. If any larvae come out on their own, place them in a sealed container with rubbing alcohol for your provider to examine.
Can my dog or cat get screwworm? +
Yes, though it is not common. If your pet has a non-healing wound with maggots, contact your veterinarian. There are now treatments specifically authorized for dogs and cats, so your vet has good options available.
I recently traveled to Mexico or Central America — should I be worried? +
Most travelers are completely fine. Keep any cuts or wounds clean and covered for about two weeks after returning, and see a doctor if a wound starts getting worse instead of better. Mentioning your travel history to the doctor helps them help you quickly.
How do I report a suspected case? +
For animals: contact your veterinarian and your state animal-health official (in Texas: Texas Animal Health Commission at tahc.texas.gov). For people: contact a healthcare provider. You can also report suspected animal cases directly to USDA APHIS at the reporting link in our Resources section. Reporting is the right thing to do — it protects your neighbors and helps officials respond fast.
